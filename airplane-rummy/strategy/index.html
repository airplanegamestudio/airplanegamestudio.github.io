Rummy strategy: going out, wild cards and what costs points

These tips come from how Airplane Rummy deals and scores a hand. Each one follows from its rules and its 116-card deck, so you can check it yourself. None of them wins every round: the draw still decides a lot.

In short

  • When you can go out, go out. A round can't score less than 0, and every turn you wait is another turn for everyone else to lower their points.
  • When two loose cards are equally little use to you, throw away the one that costs more.
  • Keep your wild cards, and get each one into a meld. A loose wild costs 20 and a loose Joker 50.
  • A pair has more cards that complete it than two cards of a run.
  • Check the meld marks before you discard: the game reads your hand from the left.

What costs points

When a round ends, only loose cards count: the cards that aren't in a set or a run. They count against you, and the lowest total after round 11 wins, so every point you are caught holding adds up.

  • High cards cost more. A loose King costs 13 and a loose 3 costs 3. When you have to give up one of two cards that do nothing for your hand, give up the higher one.
  • The wild rank changes what a card costs. In round 9, Jacks are wild, so a loose Jack costs 20, not 11. In round 11, a loose King costs 20.
  • Late rounds put more at stake. Round 1 deals 3 cards and round 11 deals 13, so a hand caught short late in the match can cost far more than one early on.

Going out

You can go out when, after your draw, every card but one fits into sets and runs. Tap that one card, then tap Go out. You score 0 for the round, and each other player gets one final turn.

  • When you can go out, go out. 0 is the lowest a round can score, so waiting can't lower your points. It only gives the others more turns to lower theirs.
  • Plan for a shape. The cards you keep are always the number dealt that round. In round 6 you keep 8, so you need one meld of 8, a 5 and a 3, or two 4s. Before each of the first seven rounds, the game lists every way to go out.
  • When someone else goes out, use your final turn. Take the face-up card if it fits a meld, or draw from the deck. Then discard your costliest loose card. If every card you keep is in a meld by then, you score 0 too.

Holding wild cards

Sixteen of the 116 cards are wild in every round: the ten cards of the round's wild rank (five suits, two of each) and the six Jokers. A wild fits almost anywhere:

  • a pair and a wild make a set, such as two 9s and a wild;
  • two cards of one suit, side by side or one apart, and a wild make a run, such as the 8 and 9 of hearts and a wild, or the 8 of hearts, a wild and the 10 of hearts;
  • two wilds and any one card make a set;
  • three wilds on their own make a set.

So keep your wilds. A wild is rarely the card that stops you going out, and the player after you can pick up whatever you discard. The computer players take a face-up wild almost every time one is offered.

The risk comes at the end of the round. A wild still loose when the round ends costs 20, and a loose Joker costs 50, more than any other card. Someone can go out on any turn, and after that you have one turn left. So put each wild into a meld as soon as you can, rather than saving it for later.

Where a wild sits matters

The game reads your hand from the left. Each meld keeps going for as long as the next card fits it, and a card counts in one meld only. So a wild placed straight after a run can join the run instead of the set you meant it for.

For example, in round 4 every 6 is wild. Your hand reads: the 8, 9 and 10 of hearts, the 6 of planes, the Jack of clubs and the Jack of spades. The wild joins the run as the Jack of hearts, and the two real Jacks are left loose: 22 points. Move the wild between the two Jacks, and you have a run of three and a set of three Jacks: 0 points.

The same goes for any card that fits the meld before it. As you arrange, the game marks each meld it counts, so check the marks before you discard. Undo puts back your last move, and there is no timer.

Which cards to keep

The deck holds two copies of every card, so each rank comes ten times: five suits, twice over. That makes a pair likelier to finish than two cards of a run. Not counting the wilds, and when none of the cards is of the wild rank:

Any of the 16 wilds completes each of them too. So when you have to break something up, keeping the pair leaves more cards that help.

Cards buried in the discard pile are out of reach: only the top card can be taken, and the rest come back only if the deck runs out and the pile is shuffled into a new deck.

Watch the table

The player after you can take your discard. The slip at your left shows that player's last turn: whether they drew face down or took the face-up card, and what they put down. If they just took a face-up 9, a 9 from you may help them too.

The computer players see their own cards and the discard pile, never your hand, so the same goes the other way: what you take from the pile tells them something, and what you draw from the deck doesn't.
